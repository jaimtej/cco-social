# How to Check the Flood Zone for Any Jacksonville Address

One of the first things we check on any home is the flood zone. It affects whether your lender requires flood insurance, what that insurance might cost, and sometimes how you feel about the house.

The good news: it's free, and it takes about two minutes. Here's how to look up any address in Duval or St. Johns County, what the letters mean, and what's changing.

## What the flood zone letters mean

FEMA's Flood Insurance Rate Maps sort land into zones. In Northeast Florida you'll mostly see these:

| Zone | What it means | Risk level |
|---|---|---|
| A, AE | 1% chance of flooding in any year. AE zones show a base flood elevation. | High |
| VE | Coastal, 1% or greater annual chance, plus the added hazard of storm waves | High |
| X (shaded) | Between the 1% and 0.2% annual chance flood areas | Moderate |
| X (unshaded) | Above the 0.2% annual chance flood level | Minimal |

High-risk zones are called Special Flood Hazard Areas. A 1% annual chance doesn't sound like much, but it works out to about a 26% chance of flooding over a 30-year mortgage.

## When flood insurance is required

If the home is in a Special Flood Hazard Area (any zone starting with A or V) and you're getting a federally backed or regulated mortgage, your lender must require flood insurance.

In Zone X, it isn't required by federal rules. But standard homeowners policies generally don't cover flooding, and FEMA says about 40% of NFIP claims come from moderate-to-low risk areas. We'd get a quote either way. Based on NFIP data, NerdWallet puts Duval's average NFIP premium at about $835 a year. We walk through that and other ownership costs in [what it really costs to own a home in Jacksonville](/blog/what-it-really-costs-to-own-a-home-jacksonville).

## Step by step: check any address

**1. Search FEMA's Flood Map Service Center.**
Go to msc.fema.gov. Type the full address in the search bar and select Search.

**2. Read the map.**
The results show a map of the location with flood zones drawn in, where digital data is available. Zoom in and find the exact lot so you're reading the right spot, especially near a zone line.

**3. Save a FIRMette.**
Select "Print Map/FIRMette" to create a free PDF of the official map for that spot, including any later map revisions. It's handy to share with your insurance agent and lender.

**4. Look for preliminary data.**
If FEMA has issued new maps that aren't official yet, you can find them on the same site by searching all products for the county. Preliminary panels can't be printed as FIRMettes because they aren't official yet, but you can still view them.

**5. In St. Johns County, use the county viewer.**
St. Johns County's Flood Zone Viewer (gis.sjcfl.us/floodviewer) lets you search by address or parcel number. It shows the adopted flood zones and base flood elevations, and whether a property has a Letter of Map Change.

**6. In Jacksonville, call for an official answer if it's close.**
For an official flood zone determination or a copy of an Elevation Certificate, the City of Jacksonville's Development Services Division can help at 904-255-8310 (214 N. Hogan St.). If a home sits near a zone line, this is worth the call.

## Duval's new maps are still preliminary

Duval County's current flood maps took effect on November 2, 2018. FEMA issued new preliminary maps for Duval County (all jurisdictions) on May 29, 2025, and published the proposed flood hazard determinations in January 2026, with comments due by April 21, 2026.

As of this writing, we haven't found an effective date for the new maps. Until they take effect, the 2018 maps are the official ones for insurance and lending. But the preliminary maps show where FEMA thinks the risk is today. That's why we look at both. If a home is moving into a high-risk zone, you'd want to know before you buy, not after.

St. Johns County's current maps took effect on December 7, 2018. Check the county viewer and FEMA's site for that county too.

## Know this too

**Florida sellers must disclose flood history.** Florida law requires sellers of residential property to give buyers a flood disclosure at or before the time the contract is signed. Since October 1, 2025, the form covers:
- Whether the seller knows of flooding that damaged the property while they owned it
- Whether they've filed a flood insurance claim, including with the NFIP
- Whether they've received federal assistance for flood damage, including from FEMA

The disclosure only covers what the seller knows and their time owning the home. It's a helpful starting point, not a full history. Ask your own questions too.

**There's usually a 30-day wait.** A new NFIP policy generally takes effect 30 days after you apply and pay. When the policy is bought as part of getting a mortgage, that wait doesn't apply, but the policy still has to be in place at closing. Either way, start quotes early. Closing week is the wrong time to find out the premium.

**Pricing is home by home.** The NFIP now sets premiums based on each property's individual flood risk, using the best available data, not just the zone letter. Two homes in the same zone can have very different premiums. Ask an insurance agent to quote the specific address.

## The bottom line

Check the flood zone before you fall in love with a house. Look at both the current and preliminary maps in Duval, use the county viewer in St. Johns, and get a flood quote even in Zone X. Read the seller's flood disclosure closely. And don't leave insurance to the last week.
