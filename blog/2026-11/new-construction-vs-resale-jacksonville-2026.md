# New Construction vs. Resale in Jacksonville: How to Compare in 2026

For years, the choice between a new build and a resale home was mostly about taste. Do you want brand-new everything, or an established street with big trees? In 2026, the math changed too.

Builders are offering more incentives than they have in years, and resale sellers are making concessions of their own. That gives buyers room to negotiate on both sides. Here's how we help clients compare the two, line by line.

## What changed this year

Builders are motivated. In the October 2026 NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index:

- **65%** of builders were offering sales incentives, the 19th straight month at 60% or higher.
- **43%** were cutting prices, the highest share at any point since 2020.
- The average price cut was 6%.
- Overall builder confidence fell to 29, its lowest reading since 2012.

Prices tell a similar story. The Census Bureau's median new home price in September 2026 was **$400,400**, while NAR's median existing-home price that month was **$417,500**. A new home costing less than a used one sounds backward. Part of the reason is that these are national medians for very different mixes of homes, in size, lot and location. So treat it as a sign that builders are pricing to sell, not as a rule for any particular house.

Resale sellers are competing too. Redfin found that 44.7% of U.S. buyers received a seller concession in August 2026. For a deeper look at timing, see [is now a good time to buy in Jacksonville?](/blog/is-now-a-good-time-to-buy-jacksonville)

## Side by side

| | New construction | Resale |
|---|---|---|
| Warranty | Builder warranty, usually tiered | Usually none, unless the seller buys a home warranty |
| Incentives | Rate buydowns, closing help, upgrades | Seller concessions, price negotiation |
| Neighborhood | Still filling in, young trees | Established streets, mature trees |
| Fees | CDD and HOA are common | Varies widely, some have none |
| Inspection | Still needed, often in phases | Standard pre-purchase inspection |

## Warranty: a real advantage, with fine print

A builder warranty is one of the biggest reasons people choose new. The Federal Trade Commission describes a common structure:

- **1 year** on workmanship and materials for most components, like drywall, paint, trim, doors and siding.
- **2 years** on HVAC, plumbing and electrical systems.
- **Up to 10 years** on major structural defects, from some builders.

Read the exclusions. The FTC notes warranties often leave out appliances, small cracks in tile, concrete or drywall, and costs like temporary housing during a major repair. Many also require mediation or arbitration instead of court. Submit repair requests in writing and keep records.

## Incentives and buydowns vs. seller concessions

Builder incentives commonly take a few forms: money toward closing costs, a mortgage rate buydown, or upgrades. They're often tied to using the builder's preferred lender. That can be a great deal, but compare it against an outside lender's quote before you commit.

On a resale home, a seller can contribute toward your closing costs or buy down your rate, too. Both builders and sellers count as "interested parties" under loan rules, so the same limits apply. On a conventional Fannie Mae loan for a primary home, contributions are capped at 3% of the price with less than 10% down, 6% with 10% to just under 25% down, and 9% with 25% or more down. Your lender will confirm what applies to you.

Two questions to ask about any buydown:

- **Is it temporary or permanent?** A temporary buydown lowers the payment for the first year or two, then it steps up.
- **What would the same money do as a price cut?** Sometimes the buydown wins. Sometimes it doesn't.

## CDD and HOA fees

Many newer Northeast Florida communities have a Community Development District (CDD) fee on the property tax bill, plus an HOA. Those can add a meaningful amount to your monthly cost, so get the exact figures in writing early. We explain how they work in [what is a CDD fee?](/blog/what-is-a-cdd-fee-jacksonville) And because your tax bill can change after you buy, read [why property taxes jump after buying in Jacksonville](/blog/why-property-taxes-jump-after-buying-jacksonville) and ask the Property Appraiser's office how your home will be assessed.

## The case for an established neighborhood

Resale homes win on things a builder can't create overnight: mature trees, finished streets and no construction traffic. You can see exactly how the neighborhood looks and how it handles a heavy rain. ## Yes, inspect the new house

A new home still goes through the city's permit inspections, but those are about code compliance. They aren't a review of workmanship on your behalf. We recommend your own independent inspector:

- **Pre-drywall,** while the framing, wiring and plumbing are still visible.
- **Final,** before closing, so items go on the builder's punch list.
- **Before the first year ends,** so anything found is still under workmanship coverage.

Ask the builder up front whether outside inspectors are allowed and when.

## Compare the true monthly payment

List price is only one piece. For each home, line up:

- Principal and interest, with and without any buydown
- Property taxes, including any CDD assessment
- Homeowners insurance and, if needed, flood insurance (see our [Jacksonville insurance guide](/blog/homeowners-insurance-jacksonville-2026))
- HOA dues
- Cash to close after incentives or concessions
- Near-term costs: window coverings, landscaping and fencing on a new build, or an older roof and HVAC on a resale

That's the number you'll live with every month.

## Bring your own agent to the model home

The sales representative in the model home is knowledgeable, but they work for the builder, and so does the contract. Incentives are easier to negotiate when someone in your corner knows what other communities and resale sellers are offering.

Many builders want your agent with you or registered at your first visit, so reach out before you tour. Many builders also offer to pay buyer agent compensation. Ask how it works in each community, and we'll put how we're paid in writing before we start.

## The bottom line

New construction and resale both have room to negotiate right now. A new build gives you a warranty and often a rate buydown. A resale home gives you an established neighborhood and concessions that can work much the same way. Neither one is automatically the better deal. The right answer comes from comparing the real monthly payment on the real homes you like.
